          ## Ribblehead Viaduct Facts
          
          - **Length:** 440 yards (about 400 metres)
          - **Height:** 104 feet (32 metres) at the highest point
          - **Arches:** 24, each with a span of 45 feet
          - **Built:** first stone laid 12 October 1870, opened to goods traffic in 1875 and passengers in 1876
          - **Engineer:** John Sydney Crossley, for the Midland Railway
          - **Also known as:** Batty Moss Viaduct
          - **Listed:** Grade II*, with the navvy camps beneath it protected as a scheduled monument
          - **Nearest station:** Ribblehead, two stops down the Settle-Carlisle line from Garsdale
          
          ## Building the Viaduct
          
          Ribblehead Viaduct took the best part of five years to build, and the men who built it lived on the moor while they did it. Something like two thousand navvies worked on the viaduct at its peak, many of them with their families, living in wooden shanty towns with names like Batty Green, Sebastopol, Belgravia and Jerusalem. The camps had their own shops, a school, a library, a post office and several pubs, and they were rough places by most accounts. The plan was originally for eighteen arches, and it was only changed to twenty-four in December 1872, when the work was already well under way.
          
          It was dangerous work, and smallpox went through the camps as well. So many people died that the little churchyard at St Leonard's in Chapel-le-Dale, a few miles down the road towards Ingleton, had to be extended, and there is a memorial plaque inside the church to the men who lost their lives building this stretch of the Settle-Carlisle. It is worth calling in if you are driving back that way. The faint outlines of Batty Green are still there on the ground between the road and the viaduct if you look for them.
          
          A century later the viaduct very nearly brought the whole line down. By the 1980s the cost of repairing Ribblehead was one of the main reasons British Rail gave for wanting to close the Settle-Carlisle, and it took years of campaigning before the line was reprieved in 1989. Our page on [Ruswarp and the Settle-Carlisle](/ruswarp-and-the-settle-carlisle/) tells the Garsdale side of that story, including the Border Collie whose statue stands on the platform at Garsdale Station.
          
          ## Getting to Ribblehead Viaduct from Garsdale
          
          **By train.** The easiest way to see Ribblehead Viaduct from our Garsdale holiday cottages is to take the train from [Garsdale Station](/places/garsdale-station/). Southbound trains towards Settle and Leeds stop at [Dent](/places/dent-station/) and then Ribblehead, and the journey takes around a quarter of an hour. Coming from Garsdale the train goes through the Blea Moor Tunnel and then straight over the viaduct just before it pulls into Ribblehead, so you get to ride across the arches on the way there, though you see more of them from the ground than from the carriage window. Ribblehead station has a small visitor centre about the line, and it is around a ten or fifteen minute walk from the platform to the foot of the viaduct.
          
          **By car.** Ribblehead is on the B6255 between Hawes and Ingleton, and from Garsdale the usual way is east to [Hawes](/locations/hawes/) on the A684 and then south over Newby Head, which takes around forty minutes. There is free roadside parking near the junction of the B6255 and the B6479, about half a mile from the viaduct on an easy gravel track. It fills up early on summer weekends because the Yorkshire Three Peaks walkers start here, and there is often a van in the lay-by selling teas and bacon sandwiches.
          
          ## Walks Around Ribblehead Viaduct
          
          The short walk most people do is from the parking area along the track to the foot of the viaduct and back, which is about a mile all told and fine for most ages. Northern's circular Ribblehead Viaduct walk from the station is about 3.6 miles and takes a couple of hours, going under the arches and up alongside the line towards Blea Moor before coming back to the road.
          
          For something longer, Whernside, the highest of the Three Peaks at 736 metres, can be climbed from Ribblehead in a circular walk of around eight miles, and the view back down onto the viaduct from the ridge is one of the better ones in the Yorkshire Dales. Ingleborough is on the other side of the road, and the full Yorkshire Three Peaks is about 24 miles if you are that way inclined. For walks closer to home, see our [walks from Garsdale](/walks/) page.
          
          ## Ribblehead with a Dog
          
          Ribblehead Viaduct is a good dog friendly day out, which matters to us because all three of our Garsdale cottages are dog friendly. The land around the viaduct is open moor grazed by sheep, so dogs need to be on a lead for most of the walk, and Northern's own guidance for the circular route says the same. The [Station Inn](https://www.stationinnribblehead.co.uk/) at Ribblehead, across the road from the station and looking straight at the viaduct, is dog friendly inside and has a beer garden. Dogs also travel free on Northern trains, so the train from Garsdale Station works with the dog as well. Our [dog friendly holiday cottages](/dog-friendly-cottages-in-garsdale-or-yorkshire-dales-stays/) page has more on walking with dogs round here.
